# One More Game

Original narration | Hardstart Films | 938 words

## 01 — arcade

The match ended at midnight. A teammate laughed through the headset. The small room felt full. Then somebody said, one more?

## 02 — clock

He looked at the time. Tomorrow, he had promised to build a birdhouse with his younger brother. There was room for one last game.

## 03 — arcade

The next match went badly. He did not want to finish on that feeling. The one after it was close. He did not want to leave while they were finding their rhythm.

## 04 — bed

When he finally stopped, the room was silent again. He set three alarms and pictured himself waking as someone who had slept enough.

## 05 — phone

At nine twenty, the phone vibrated. His brother had sent a photo of an empty workbench. No accusation. Just two pieces of wood, a pencil, and a seat he had not filled.

## 06 — street

He arrived after the session ended. The caretaker was stacking chairs. His brother carried the unfinished pieces carefully home in a bag.

## 07 — bench

His explanation sounded thin while he was saying it. His brother nodded. We can do it another time. That made excuses harder.

## 08 — workbench

Back home, the bag went beside the desk. The unfinished birdhouse made no sound. It simply waited while the game loaded.

## 09 — arcade

He liked the game. He liked his friends there. A good evening on a screen meant something to him.

## 10 — notes

The problem was the distance between the evening he chose and the morning he had promised somebody else. He had been letting one decision make the other without admitting the connection.

## 11 — diagram

He drew three boxes: one more match, less sleep, a broken promise. It was the sequence of that Saturday, not an argument that every person who played would live the same story.

## 12 — phone

He opened his brother's message again. For a minute, he planned a grand apology, followed by a perfect new schedule. Then he deleted the speech and asked when they could meet.

## 13 — workbench

Thursday after school, his brother replied. He wrote the time on paper and placed it under the keyboard. The promise now had a place his eyes would meet.

## 14 — arcade

That night, he told his teammates he would leave at eleven. One joked about it. Another said he had an early shift too. The boundary felt less dramatic once it was spoken aloud.

## 15 — clock

At ten forty, a match ended. A new one would run past eleven. His hand moved toward the button anyway. Reading the clock was easy. Accepting an unfinished appetite was harder.

## 16 — arcade

He wanted a satisfying ending: a win, a rank, a reason that would make stopping feel obvious. The game did not owe him that ending. He would have to choose one himself.

## 17 — phoneaway

He took off the headset and said goodnight before his hand could start another queue. His friends continued without him. It felt like standing outside a lit room.

## 18 — kitchen

He went to the kitchen and made something to eat. The apartment had no scoreboard. Nobody praised him for washing a plate. He washed it because he would need it tomorrow.

## 19 — workbench

Then he opened the bag. He spread the wood on the floor and checked what was missing. Sandpaper, two small hinges, and someone who would actually arrive when he said he would.

## 20 — street

Thursday arrived with rain. On the way to the workshop, he wanted to go home. Keeping a promise did not make the bus faster or the weather more encouraging.

## 21 — workbench

His brother was already there, turning a piece of wood over in his hands. This time, the second chair stayed occupied. They measured the base and discovered their first mistake together.

## 22 — write

One side was too short. He reached for a new piece. His brother suggested changing the design instead. They argued, tried it, and ended up with a roof that leaned slightly to one side.

## 23 — diagram

The second diagram had no scores: choose a stopping time, protect the next commitment, show up. It was a plan for his own week, not a demand that he give up everything he enjoyed.

## 24 — workbench

The real afternoon was slower than a game. Tools needed putting away. Glue needed time. There was no button that skipped the awkward part where neither of them knew what to do next.

## 25 — phone

While the glue dried, his phone lit up. His teammates were online early. He read the message, answered that he would join later, and put the phone back in his pocket.

## 26 — bench

His brother noticed. He did not say anything about discipline. He asked whether the doorway was wide enough for a bird. They measured it, then made the opening a little larger.

## 27 — arcade

On Friday, he played again. He enjoyed it. At the agreed time, stopping still felt disappointing. He left anyway, without pretending disappointment meant the evening had been a mistake.

## 28 — clock

Another night, he stayed too late. The next day felt heavy. He did not turn that into proof that boundaries were pointless. He looked at the moment he had stopped checking the time.

## 29 — notes

He moved the reminder outside the game and asked a friend to say goodbye with him. It would not make every choice easy. It made the choice harder to lose inside another match.

## 30 — workbench

By the next weekend, the birdhouse was finished. Crooked roof, uneven paint, working hinges. His brother opened the little panel twice, simply because they had made something that opened.

## 31 — street

They hung it outside the window. No bird arrived on cue. They stood there anyway, looking at the small wooden thing in the rain, with nothing to unlock and nowhere to rank.

## 32 — end

He did not need to stop enjoying the world on the screen. He needed to leave enough of himself for the world that kept his promises. The next invitation came. He checked tomorrow first.

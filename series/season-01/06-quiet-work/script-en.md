# Nobody Needs to Know

Hardstart Films — English narration script

## Beat 01 — workbench

He announced the chair before cutting any wood. He posted a sketch, a photograph of his tools, and a sentence about finally becoming a maker.

## Beat 02 — phone

People were kind. They liked the sketch. Asked when it would be finished. For one evening, he felt as though he had already built something worth sitting on.

## Beat 03 — kitchen

The chair was for his mother. Her kitchen stool rocked. Each visit, she folded another square of cardboard underneath and changed the subject.

## Beat 04 — workbench

He wanted to replace it with something beautiful. A curved back. Carefully joined legs. A piece that looked as if it belonged in the photographs he had saved.

## Beat 05 — notes

He had built one shelf. It leaned. The sketch did not show that. Online, he had already become a more experienced maker.

## Beat 06 — workbench

The first joint did not fit. He pushed harder, split the edge, and turned the damaged side away from the window. He checked his phone before he examined the mistake.

## Beat 07 — phone

Another message arrived. How is the chair coming along? He photographed the good side of the wood and replied, Making progress. The sentence was technically true and carefully incomplete.

## Beat 08 — organize

He arranged the workshop for another photograph. The unfinished parts resembled work from a distance. The broken joint stayed outside the frame.

## Beat 09 — window

When the light disappeared, he had a clean workshop and the same broken joint. His mother's stool was still rocking. The photographs could not carry weight on her kitchen floor.

## Beat 10 — diagram

He could see the difference now: intention, announcement, applause. Then the object, still untouched. The response was real. It simply was not evidence that the chair had been made.

## Beat 11 — workbench

He avoided the room. His imagined audience felt louder than the real people encouraging him. He dreaded questions nobody had asked cruelly.

## Beat 12 — bench

On Saturday, he went to the community workshop. An older maker watched him turn the damaged piece in his hands and asked what he had tried. He almost pretended to know.

## Beat 13 — notes

Instead, he admitted that he did not understand the joint. The admission made him feel smaller for a moment. Then, for the first time, someone could help with the actual problem.

## Beat 14 — workbench

They looked at a simpler design. He disliked it at first. It would not resemble the photograph he had posted. But it matched what he could learn and finish responsibly.

## Beat 15 — kitchen

He called his mother and asked what she needed. A stable stool, she said. Nothing fancy. Something easy to move while she cooked. He had never asked her before drawing the chair.

## Beat 16 — notes

He changed the plan: fewer parts, a comfortable height, a shape suited to her kitchen. The project became less impressive and more specific.

## Beat 17 — phoneaway

Then he put the phone on a shelf. He did not delete the account or announce that he was working in silence. He simply left the camera off for the afternoon.

## Beat 18 — workbench

He practiced on scrap wood. The first attempt was rough. The second was also rough, but in a different place. He marked the edges so he could remember what had changed.

## Beat 19 — diagram

The new loop was less glamorous: make, test, ask, revise. It needed other people, but for feedback rather than applause. Each pass left something useful on the bench.

## Beat 20 — clock

He did not spend every spare hour there. Some evenings, he cooked and went to bed. Consistency still had to fit a life.

## Beat 21 — workbench

On the days he worked, he began with the last note he had left himself. Check this fit. Ask about that edge. A small record kept him from restarting the whole argument.

## Beat 22 — phone

A friend messaged. He sent the failed practice pieces. Learning this part, he wrote. The friend answered with a useful question.

## Beat 23 — bench

At the workshop, he asked someone to inspect the assembled stool. They found a problem he had missed. He felt the familiar rush to defend it, then took the stool apart.

## Beat 24 — workbench

Repairing the problem cost him a weekend. Hiding it would have been faster. But his mother would be the person using the stool, not the people scrolling past a photograph.

## Beat 25 — notes

He kept a page of corrections beside the bench. Dates, questions, changes. It was ugly and practical. He began to trust it.

## Beat 26 — window

There were afternoons when the workshop felt quiet in a bad way. Without a new post, nobody knew he was there. He noticed how much he wanted proof that the effort counted.

## Beat 27 — workbench

Then he ran his hand over a smooth edge and felt a place he had missed. He marked it. The work had an answer for his attention even when the internet did not.

## Beat 28 — calendar

The project took longer than promised. He told the people who asked. He was learning, and learning was taking time. Most understood.

## Beat 29 — workbench

Eventually, the pieces came together. He had the finished stool checked again before giving it away. He wanted confidence built from inspection, not from how much he wanted it to be finished.

## Beat 30 — kitchen

He carried it into the kitchen without a camera in his hand. His mother moved the old stool aside. The folded cardboard stayed on the floor, suddenly unnecessary.

## Beat 31 — bench

She sat, shifted her weight, and reached for a bowl. Then she asked him to pass the onions. The moment was smaller than the reveal he had imagined. It was also the point.

## Beat 32 — phone

Later, he asked permission for a photograph. He shared the stool alongside his practice pieces. This time, there was something real to share.

## Beat 33 — workbench

He still liked encouragement. He still needed advice. The change was not that nobody could know. It was that being seen had stopped taking the place of doing the work.

## Beat 34 — end

Your next effort does not need an audience before it needs your hands. Tell someone when you need help. Ask for honest feedback. Let the work become real between the announcements.

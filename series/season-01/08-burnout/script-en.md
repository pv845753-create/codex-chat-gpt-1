# You Can’t Outwork Empty

English narration. 949 words. Timing follows the voice recording.

## 1 — workbench

He repaired lamps in a workshop. The bench stayed bright long after closing. He felt dimmer every morning.

## 2 — clock

The extra hours had a reason. A late delivery. A bill. A customer who needed a lamp for a shop opening. He could manage one difficult week.

## 3 — calendar

Another week arrived. He erased the end time from his schedule and wrote a new promise beside it. The promise always belonged to someone else.

## 4 — workbench

He skipped lunch to finish a repair. When his hands shook, he held the screwdriver tighter. When he made a mistake, he stayed later to correct it.

## 5 — kitchen

He ate standing beside the sink. The food cooled while he answered messages. He could not remember whether he had tasted it or simply made it disappear.

## 6 — phone

Friends stopped asking him to come out, then asked less often how he was doing. He called that focus. Some part of him was relieved not to explain himself.

## 7 — bed

In bed, he counted unfinished orders. When he finally slept, the alarm seemed to arrive immediately. He began the next morning already feeling behind.

## 8 — notes

On the bench sat a note he had written months earlier: work harder. The words had once sounded encouraging. Now they were the only answer he allowed himself.

## 9 — mirror

He assumed tiredness was a character flaw. Other people appeared to manage more. He compared their visible output with the part of his own life that was coming apart.

## 10 — workbench

One afternoon, he connected a wire incorrectly. The lamp stayed dark. He stared at it for a long time.

## 11 — clock

He had spent an hour repeating a ten minute repair. Adding another hour suddenly seemed less like solving the problem and more like continuing it.

## 12 — diagram

His evenings had become a closed path: accept another job, borrow from rest, struggle with the work, then borrow more time. The diagram described his week, not a medical diagnosis.

## 13 — window

He opened the workshop window. Across the street, a worker locked a door and left. An ordinary gesture. He realized he could not remember the last time leaving had felt ordinary.

## 14 — phone

When his sister called, he nearly let it ring out. Instead, he answered. She asked a simple question. Have you eaten? He could not make a joke come quickly enough.

## 15 — kitchen

They met at a cafe. He tried to explain the orders first. Then he said what he had been avoiding: I am exhausted, and I cannot seem to switch off.

## 16 — bench

She did not tell him to become tougher. She asked what was urgent, what was promised, and what he was afraid would happen if he stopped for one evening.

## 17 — notes

Some things were genuinely urgent. Some had become urgent because he had agreed too quickly. Some customers had never asked for the deadline he had silently imposed on himself.

## 18 — phone

He sent two uncomfortable messages about revised collection times. One customer was disappointed. Another said next week was fine. Neither response solved everything, but both were real information.

## 19 — workbench

He finished the repair already open on the bench. Then he unplugged the tools, covered the loose parts, and wrote down where to resume. Stopping needed a procedure too.

## 20 — door

At six thirty, he locked the workshop. His shoulders stayed tense as he walked away. He kept expecting the phone to announce that leaving had been a terrible mistake.

## 21 — water

At home, he drank water and ate a meal. The quiet felt unfamiliar. He reached for work twice. He put it down twice, without pretending that resting felt easy.

## 22 — bed

He did not wake up transformed. One evening could not repair everything. He was still tired, and that fact deserved attention rather than another punishment.

## 23 — diagram

He made room for a different pattern: notice the strain, reduce what could be reduced, and protect recovery. A set of choices, not a guarantee or a cure.

## 24 — calendar

He marked an end time again. He left space between jobs instead of treating every empty square as a failure. Then he checked whether the plan could actually pay the bills.

## 25 — notes

The numbers required changes. He reviewed prices, delivery promises, and the repairs he accepted. A sustainable workshop could not depend on pretending his time and body had no limits.

## 26 — workbench

Some afternoons, he caught himself rushing. He checked his hands, put the tool down, and took a break before a small error became an evening of repairs.

## 27 — phone

The exhaustion did not disappear neatly. When sleep and concentration kept troubling him, he arranged an appointment with a qualified professional. He did not need to earn permission to ask for help.

## 28 — window

He also told his sister when the week became difficult again. Not every conversation ended with a solution. Being less alone with it still changed how the evening felt.

## 29 — workbench

A customer brought in a brass lamp. He explained the realistic waiting time before accepting it. Saying the limit aloud felt uncomfortable. The customer agreed.

## 30 — clock

At closing time, the lamp was unfinished. He labeled the parts and stopped. For once, unfinished work was evidence of a plan continuing tomorrow, not proof that he had failed today.

## 31 — street

Outside, the sky was still light. He heard a bus pull away and somebody laughing near the corner. The city contained things that had nothing to do with his order book.

## 32 — workbench

Over time, some days became easier. Others were still hard. He kept changing what did not work, including promises that sounded reasonable until he tried to live inside them.

## 33 — notes

He took down the old note above the bench. In its place, he wrote something less impressive: check the load. It reminded him to look at conditions before blaming himself.

## 34 — end

You cannot outwork empty forever. If your effort is consuming the person making it, that deserves care, support, and practical change. The workshop can wait while you notice the light.

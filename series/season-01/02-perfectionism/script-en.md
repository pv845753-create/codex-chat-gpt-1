# The Draft You Never Finish

Hardstart Films — original English screenplay.

## 01 — workbench

The poster was finished on Tuesday. By Wednesday, June had decided the moon was too large. By Thursday, she had decided the entire sky was wrong.

## 02 — notes

Twenty-three versions lay across her studio floor. Each solved something in the previous one. Each seemed to reveal a new mistake she had somehow failed to see.

## 03 — workbench

The poster was for a small neighborhood exhibition. Nothing prestigious. A friend had asked her to bring one print. The deadline was Saturday, and she had said yes.

## 04 — projector

In her head, however, the room had become enormous. Every visitor would stop at her corner. Every weakness in the drawing would say something permanent about her.

## 05 — workbench

June was not avoiding the work. She worked constantly. She sharpened pencils to needles, mixed another color, and scraped yesterday's certainty from the edge of the printing block.

## 06 — clock

At midnight, the lamp still burned. She changed the spacing between two letters, stepped back, and waited for the feeling that would tell her she could stop.

## 07 — notes

The feeling never came. A clean edge made a rough one noticeable. A better shadow made the lettering seem weak. Improvement kept moving the finish line.

## 08 — street

On Friday, she carried a test print to the frame shop. Rain freckled the protective sleeve. She held it against her chest as if it could hear her doubts.

## 09 — workbench

The framer asked whether it was the final version. June opened her mouth and realized she had no clear definition of final, only a list of reasons it was not.

## 10 — notes

He handed her a scrap of card to record the dimensions. She wrote the exhibition title instead. Then the paper size. Those were facts she could actually answer.

## 11 — diagram

Back home, she separated the brief from the moving target. The brief asked for a readable title, a clear scene, and the agreed size. Her fear asked for universal approval.

## 12 — workbench

Universal approval had not been part of the commission. It had slipped in quietly and taken control of every decision. No amount of careful printing could guarantee it.

## 13 — books

She opened an old sketchbook. The drawings were clumsy in places, but she remembered making them. A crooked roof carried more life than some of her polished recent work.

## 14 — notes

This did not mean mistakes were secretly better. It meant a picture could have something worth seeing before every problem in it had disappeared. She had forgotten that distinction.

## 15 — workbench

June set a test print beneath the lamp. She read the title from the doorway. She checked the paper size. She looked for the shape that carried the story.

## 16 — door

The title was readable. The size was right. The shape was clear. Her stomach still tightened. The checks had settled practical questions, not provided immunity from being judged.

## 17 — clock

She chose one final working session. Enough time to repair the broken letter and clean a distracting mark. Not enough time to begin the entire sky again.

## 18 — workbench

When the new color tempted her, she covered its jar. When another idea arrived, she wrote it on a separate sheet. A future possibility did not have to become tonight's emergency.

## 19 — notes

The repair went badly once. A red line spread beyond its intended edge. She felt the familiar surge: start over. Instead, she blotted the ink and inspected the damage.

## 20 — workbench

A small border could absorb the mark. She tried it on spare paper, then on the print. The result differed from the image in her head. It still worked.

## 21 — diagram

This time, her process had an exit: define the piece, make it, check the agreed needs, release it. New ideas could return in the next piece, instead of trapping this one.

## 22 — organize

She cleaned the tools and wrapped the block. Each object leaving the table made her uneasy. Tidying was no longer preparation for more work. It was evidence that work could end.

## 23 — phone

Then she sent her friend a photograph and the frame dimensions. Before she could add three paragraphs of apology, a reply arrived: great, see you tomorrow.

## 24 — bed

She slept badly anyway. In the dark, she redesigned the sky twice. The urge to revise did not respect the fact that the ink was dry and the poster was packed.

## 25 — street

On Saturday, she walked to the exhibition with both hands around the frame. The package felt heavier whenever she imagined turning back. She kept walking without pretending to feel brave.

## 26 — stage

The room was smaller than the one in her head. A table rocked on an uneven floor. Someone was searching for tape. Her corner had a slightly crooked hook.

## 27 — workbench

June hung the print. From that distance, she immediately saw a flaw in the border. Her hand lifted toward it, although there was nothing she could fix without taking everything down.

## 28 — bench

A visitor paused and asked about the small figure beneath the moon. June explained the scene. The visitor told her what it reminded him of. He had not mentioned the border.

## 29 — mirror

Another person passed without stopping. That hurt more than she expected. Then she watched someone pass a painting she admired. Attention was uneven; it was not a final measurement of worth.

## 30 — notes

She wrote down two useful observations after the exhibition. Make the lettering stronger next time. Leave room for a lighter shadow. Improvement finally had somewhere to go that was not backward.

## 31 — workbench

On Sunday, she put a fresh sheet on the table. The finished poster leaned against the wall, imperfect and complete. Beside it, another piece was allowed to begin.

## 32 — end

Care about the work. Give it a clear brief. Correct what matters. But do not make one unfinished piece responsible for proving that you deserve to make the next one.
